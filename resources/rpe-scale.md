# RPE Scale

RPE (rate of perceived exertion) is a 1–10 rating of how hard a set felt. In lifting it doubles as an estimate of how many reps were left before failure: **RPE = 10 − reps in reserve (RIR)**.

## The scale

- **10** — nothing left; another rep wasn't possible
- **9** — one more rep was possible
- **8** — two more reps were possible
- **7** — three more reps were possible
- **6** — four or more reps were possible

Half points cover the in-between cases:

- **9.5** — probably no more reps, but maybe one
- **8.5** — one more rep for sure, maybe two
- **7.5** — two more reps for sure, maybe three

Anything below about 6 is warm-up territory and is usually left unrated.

## Where it comes from

The idea traces back to the Swedish researcher Gunnar Borg, who built a 6–20 exertion scale in the 1960s to track effort during cardio. The 1–10 lifting version used today was popularized by powerlifting coach Mike Tuchscherer in the mid-2000s.

## Things to keep in mind

- **It's subjective.** Lifters often misjudge their reserve by a rep or so, and the error tends to be larger on high-rep sets and isolation exercises. A rating is most useful when the same person rates the same way over time, so trends matter more than any single number.
- **A common guideline** is to keep most working sets around RPE 7–8, two or three reps shy of failure, which builds muscle with less fatigue than taking every set to failure. RPE 9.5–10 is usually saved for the last set of an exercise or a deliberate top set. Coaches differ on this; the trainer decides what's right for the program.
- **Context shifts the number.** Sleep, stress, food, heat, and time off can all move how a given weight feels, so the same load can land at different RPEs on different days.
